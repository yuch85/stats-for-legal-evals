Audio

Eighteen episodes of statistics come down to one page. This finale takes the contract platform’s whole journey — from the Episode 1 pilot to the rollout decision — and writes it up as a one-page eval report: score, interval, n, test, effect, cost, limits, version. Then it covers what the report cannot end with: the cost trade-off, the workflow test, the monitoring that follows rollout, and the cautionary tales about what happens when a report hides its limits.

The One-Page Eval Report

A reader of an eval report — a partner, a vendor, a regulator — needs to see exactly what the evaluation proves and what it does not, without reading twenty pages. One page forces the choice: every number on it must be justified, and every limit must be stated. The template below has eight fields. A report that leaves one out is incomplete, and the missing field is usually the one that would have changed the decision.

Field What it carries Why it is there
ScoreThe headline metric, with its definition and denominatorA score without a denominator is not a number (Episode 8)
IntervalThe 95% interval around the scoreThe width is the news (Episode 6)
nThe number of items, and the number of clusters they came fromQuestions from the same document travel together (Episode 13)
TestThe test used, and whether it was pre-specifiedAn unplanned test is exploration, not confirmation (Episode 18)
EffectThe difference against the baseline, with its interval, against the department’s minimum important differenceSignificant is not the same as worth anything (Episode 10)
CostCost per item and per hundred items, and review timeA cheaper build can win, and an accurate one can lose
LimitsWhat the test set did not cover, what was not measured, what could go wrongThe reader cannot weigh what is not stated
VersionModel, prompt, test set, and dateWithout a version, the report cannot be repeated

Two rules govern the template. First, the interval travels with the score everywhere the score goes — in the summary, in the table, in the slide shown to the partner. A score quoted without its interval in the executive summary and with the interval buried on page nine is a score quoted without its interval. Second, the limits section is not a disclaimer paragraph at the end. It is a field of the report, as load-bearing as the score: it names the document types not tested, the subgroups too small to judge, and the conditions under which the result would not hold.

Worked Example: The Contract Platform’s Report

Read the report as a decision document. The score says the builds are close. The interval says the paired gap is positive but its size is uncertain. The effect field says the estimate is above the department’s minimum important difference while the interval straddles it, so the data cannot settle it and the average does not decide. The limits say the pilot covered two document types out of five. The decision the report supports is a conditional rollout: AI-assisted first-pass review for NDAs under human verification, MSAs kept under closer supervision, and the next test round covering the three untested document types before any wider rollout.

Cost and the Pareto Frontier

Accuracy without cost is half a procurement decision. The Pareto frontier is the set of options no other option beats on both accuracy and cost: an option is on the frontier if nothing else is both cheaper and at least as accurate. An option that another option beats on both is dominated, and a dominated option should not be bought at any price — the option that dominates it gives more for less.

Four illustrative builds, priced per hundred documents:

Build Accuracy Cost per 100 docs Verdict
C (cheap)78%$120On the frontier: cheapest
A (current)85%$400On the frontier
D84%$650Dominated by A: dearer and worse
B (premium)86%$900On the frontier: dearest, best

The frontier runs through C, A, and B. D sits below it: Build A is cheaper than D and more accurate, so D is dominated.

The frontier does not choose between C, A, and B — it narrows the choice to them. Choosing among frontier options needs the department’s minimum important difference (Episode 10): is the seven-point gain from C to A worth $280 per hundred documents, and is the one-point gain from A to B worth $500? Cost in the report is not the licence fee alone. It includes verification time: a build that needs a lawyer to re-check every output can cost more in lawyer hours than it saves in drafting time, even at a low licence fee. Report total cost per hundred documents, licence plus verification, or the comparison misleads.

Workflow Evaluation: Lawyer-Only Against Lawyer-Plus-AI

Model-only benchmark scores are not evidence that the deployed workflow improves outcomes. A benchmark scores the model’s output. A workflow scores the lawyer’s output after using the model — including the time spent checking it, the errors the lawyer catches, the errors the lawyer misses because the model’s confident draft anchored them, and the rework when the draft has to be redone. The workflow evaluation is a controlled or crossover exercise:

  • Comparable tasks. Lawyers perform comparable matters with and without the tool — matched on document type, length, and complexity, so the comparison is fair.
  • Crossover. Each lawyer does some matters with the tool and some without, so differences between lawyers cancel out. Matters are not repeated across conditions for the same lawyer: seeing a matter once changes how it is done the second time.
  • Blinded grading. The work is graded without the grader knowing which condition produced it, on a rubric the graders agreed in advance (Episode 15).
  • Consequential errors counted separately. A missed material deviation is not the same as a stylistic infelicity. Count the errors that would change the advice or the client’s position.
  • Total time, including verification. Completion time plus verification time, rework, and escalation rates — the hours the tool actually costs, not the hours it appears to save.

The illustrative numbers below show the pattern the exercise is designed to catch. Twelve lawyers, each completing six comparable matters, three with the tool and three without:

Measure Lawyer-only Lawyer-plus-AI Reading
Blinded quality (out of 10)7.67.8Slightly higher with the tool
Consequential errors per 100 matters32One fewer per hundred
Total time per matter (incl. verification)95 min62 min33 minutes saved
Matters needing rework8%9%No meaningful change

Two conclusions follow from the structure, not from any single number. First, a tool with slightly lower standalone accuracy can still win: if it saves substantial time without increasing material errors, the workflow is better even though the benchmark score is worse. Second, a highly accurate tool can lose: if it creates so much checking work that it provides little practical benefit — long drafts that must be verified line by line, confident errors that take longer to catch than to write from scratch — the workflow is worse even though the benchmark score is better. The workflow evaluation is what separates those two cases. A report that quotes only the benchmark score has not evaluated the deployment at all.

Post-Deployment Monitoring

The finale used to end at the rollout decision. Monitoring is what follows it. A model that scored well in the pilot can degrade in production: the document mix changes, the vendor ships an update, the prompt drifts, the law changes. Monitoring answers one question each week: is the tool still performing as the report said it would?

How many outputs to spot-check each week. Spot-checking is sampling, and the sample size follows the same logic as Episode 9: how small an error rate must you be able to see? If the true error rate is 5%, the chance of seeing at least one error in a spot-check of n outputs is 1 − 0.95n. For n = 20: 0.9520 ≈ 0.36, so the chance is about 64%. For n = 30: about 79%. For n = 60: 0.9560 ≈ 0.046, so the chance is about 95%. A weekly spot-check of 60 outputs gives a 95% chance of catching at least one error when the true rate is 5%. A weekly spot-check of 20 outputs misses the problem more than a third of the time. Pick the number from the error rate you need to detect, and state it in the monitoring plan.

Control charts. Plot the weekly error rate on a chart with a centre line at the baseline rate and control limits three standard errors either side. For a baseline error rate p = 0.05 and a weekly spot-check of n = 100 outputs, the standard error is √(0.05 × 0.95 ÷ 100) = √0.000475 ≈ 0.0218. The upper control limit is 0.05 + 3 × 0.0218 ≈ 0.115, and the lower limit is 0.05 − 0.065, floored at zero. A week above 11.5% errors is a signal: investigate before the next week’s batch goes out. A single week inside the limits is not a signal, even if the rate looks high — that is what the limits are for.

Seven weeks inside the limits, then week eight above the upper control limit. That week is the signal — not the wobble in weeks two and six.

Drift and the probe set. Keep a fixed probe set — the same documents, re-run every month — so a change in the score means the tool changed, not the workload. When the probe score moves, or the control chart signals, the response is written down in advance: pause new rollouts, re-run the evaluation on the current version, and check whether the vendor changed the model, the prompt, or the retrieval. Monitoring without a pre-written response is a chart nobody acts on.

Measurement Validity: Are We Measuring the Legal Quality We Care About?

Validity asks whether the metric measures the legal quality it claims to measure. A rubric can be reliable — graders agree, scores are stable — and still measure the wrong thing. Three checks belong in the report:

  • Does the rubric track consequential errors? A rubric that rewards fluent drafting and ignores a missed indemnity cap gives high scores to dangerous outputs. Score consequential errors separately, and weight them by consequence, not by count.
  • Does the test set resemble the work? A test set of template NDAs validates template NDAs (Episode 3). A report that generalises from it to bespoke MSAs has over-claimed, whatever its p-value.
  • Would a better score mean better advice? A statistically significant improvement in a poorly designed rubric is not a meaningful improvement. If the rubric cannot distinguish a good answer from a polished wrong one, the improvement is in the rubric, not in the law.

Validity is checked by people, not by statistics: a senior lawyer reads a sample of outputs the rubric scored highly and asks whether they would send them to a client. If the answer is no, the rubric needs redesigning before the next round, and the report says so.

Versioning

An eval result belongs to a version, not to a product. Record all four, on the report itself: the model version, the prompt version, the test-set version, and the date. A vendor update that changes the model invalidates the old score until it is re-run; a prompt edit does the same. Keep the test set frozen for the comparison round and version it when it changes, so a later reader can tell whether a score moved because the tool improved or because the test got easier. The one-page report carries the versions in its Version field. The full record — the prompt text, the test-set list, the grader instructions, the analysis plan — is filed with the report and referenced from it, so the evaluation can be repeated from the file alone.

Three Reports, One Template

The litigation report. A dismissal-prediction tool, evaluated on LegalForecastBench: 91 US federal cases, 387 claim–defendant units, dismissal base rate 73.6%. The report’s score is the micro-Brier score (Episode 14); the best model on the benchmark scores 0.118, against 0.194 for always predicting the base rate and 0.25 for constant 0.5 forecasts. The limits field carries the selection effect: litigated cases that reach a motion to dismiss are not a random sample of disputes, so the score describes those units, not litigation in general. The version field carries the model, the prompt, and the benchmark version.

The TAR validation report. A technology-assisted review system flags 1,000 of 10,000 documents as responsive and excludes 9,000. Lawyers take a random sample of 400 excluded documents; 12 are actually responsive. The elusion rate is 12 ÷ 400 = 3%, with a 95% Wilson interval of about 1.7% to 5.2% (Episode 8). Estimated missed responsive documents: 0.03 × 9,000 ≈ 270. Estimated recall: 1,000 ÷ (1,000 + 270) ≈ 79%, with an interval of roughly 68% to 87%. The report states the sampling method, the interval, and the limit that a low observed elusion rate does not establish zero missed documents: with 0 responsive in 400 sampled, the rule of three still allows up to about 0.75%.

The cautionary tales. Guha et al. (PNAS 2026) warn that benchmark scores can overstate what a system will do in real legal work: a score earned on a benchmark’s test set, under the benchmark’s conditions, is evidence about that test set, not a certificate for deployment. Harvey LAB (v1.4.0) shows the scale a serious benchmark reaches — 2,010 tasks, 27 practice areas, about 114,000 rubric criteria, dual judges, all-pass grading, a hallucination check — and its headline still needs its grading rule beside it: all-pass grading means one failed criterion fails the task, so the headline depends on the strictness of the rule as much as on the model. In both cases the lesson is the same: report what was measured, under what conditions, with what limits. The report that does that is useful even when the score is modest. The report that hides its limits is a demo with better stationery.

Before You Send the Report

  • Every number has its interval, its denominator, and its version beside it.
  • The primary test was pre-specified, and the exploratory findings are labelled as exploratory.
  • Subgroup results are reported for every subgroup tested, with the correction applied.
  • Cost includes verification time, and the frontier — not a single option — is shown.
  • The workflow evaluation is reported alongside the benchmark score, or its absence is stated as a limit.
  • The monitoring plan names the weekly spot-check number, the control limits, and the response to a signal.
  • A senior lawyer has read a sample of high-scoring outputs and would send them to a client.
  • The limits section names what was not tested, what was not measured, and what would change the conclusion.

Conclusion

A report that hides its limits is a demo with better stationery. The one-page report — score, interval, n, test, effect, cost, limits, version — is the whole series in one page: every technique in Episodes 1 to 18 has a field where it lands. Write it so a reader can see exactly what it proves, and what it does not. Then keep watching after rollout, because the report is a snapshot and the tool keeps changing.

That is the series. Nineteen episodes, one discipline: say what you measured, say how sure you are, and say what you did not measure.
