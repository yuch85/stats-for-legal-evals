Episode 17 — What the Model Asks Matters More

Episode 17 · Module 4 — Scoring and Agreement · Audio 6:45 · Statistics for Legal Evals

Audio

A benchmark report says the models that ask more questions score higher. Is that true, and if it is true, what does it prove? In this episode, Sera and Ben work through the correlation between asking and scoring on SG-LegalClarify, a Singapore-law benchmark — and show why the number that matters is not how often a model asks, but what it asks about.

The Setting: SG-LegalClarify

SG-LegalClarify is a benchmark of 1,100 lawyer-authored Singapore-law questions across 10 domains. Of those questions, 340 are fully specified and 760 are underspecified — they leave out facts a lawyer would need before answering — giving 1,860 instances in total. Nineteen large language models were tested on it, closed-book and with web search. The benchmark tests two things at once: legal reasoning, and whether a model recognises when a question is underspecified — whether it asks for the missing facts instead of guessing.

In the benchmark’s multi-turn pipeline, the model decides whether to answer or ask for more facts. A user simulator reveals hidden facts only when asked. Two measures come out of that pipeline, and the whole episode turns on telling them apart:

  • Clarification rate — the share of questions on which the model asks for more facts at all. It measures how often the model asks.
  • Variable coverage — the share of the hidden facts (the variables the question leaves out) that the model actually asks about. It measures what the model asks about: whether its questions target the facts that matter.

A model can have a high clarification rate and low variable coverage: it asks often, but it asks about the wrong things. A model can also ask rarely and still cover the variables that matter on the questions where it does ask. The benchmark’s headline finding is that coverage, not frequency, tracks the score. The rest of this episode is the statistics behind that sentence.

Correlation: Pearson’s r

Pearson’s r measures how closely two numbers move together in a straight line. It runs from −1 to +1. An r of +1 means the two numbers rise together in an exact straight line; −1 means one rises exactly as the other falls; 0 means no straight-line relationship is visible. The formula divides the covariance of the two variables by the product of their standard deviations:

r = covariance(x, y) ÷ (σx × σy)

  • x and y are the two variables being compared — here, variable coverage (x) and benchmark score (y) for each model.
  • σx and σy are the standard deviations of x and y: how far a typical value sits from its mean.
  • Covariance(x, y) is the average of the products of the two deviations from their means: for each model, (x − mean of x) × (y − mean of y), averaged. When models above average on x also tend to be above average on y, the products are mostly positive and r is positive.

Dividing by the two standard deviations is what puts r on the −1 to +1 scale. Without that division, the number would depend on the units of x and y. With it, an r of 0.73 means the same thing whether the variables are percentages, points, or dollars.

Three cautions belong with the definition, before any number appears. First, r measures straight-line relationships only: two variables can be strongly related in a curve and still have a low r. Second, r is a single number summarising a cloud of points, so always look at the scatter plot beside it — one outlying model can pull r a long way. Third, r says nothing about cause. That last point gets its own section below.

The means first. Coverage: (40 + 55 + 65 + 75 + 90) ÷ 5 = 325 ÷ 5 = 65. Score: (55 + 60 + 79 + 84 + 72) ÷ 5 = 350 ÷ 5 = 70. The deviation columns subtract those means. Model E is the instructive row: the highest coverage in the set, 90%, but a score of 72%, barely above the mean of 70% — its product is only 50, because its score deviation is only 2.

Now the three sums that make r:

  • Sum of the products: 375 + 100 + 0 + 140 + 50 = 665.
  • Sum of the squared coverage deviations: (−25)² + (−10)² + 0² + 10² + 25² = 625 + 100 + 0 + 100 + 625 = 1,450.
  • Sum of the squared score deviations: (−15)² + (−10)² + 9² + 14² + 2² = 225 + 100 + 81 + 196 + 4 = 606.

Then r = 665 ÷ √(1,450 × 606) = 665 ÷ √878,700 = 665 ÷ 937.4 ≈ 0.71. The positive sign says coverage and score rise together across these five models. The size, 0.71, says the straight-line relationship is strong but far from exact — Model E is the visible exception.

Each dot is one model. The dashed line is the straight line that best fits the dots. Model E sits well below it: the highest coverage, a middling score.

r-Squared: How Much of the Spread Is Accounted For

Squaring r gives r², the share of the variation in one variable that is accounted for by its straight-line relationship with the other. For the five-model example, r² = 0.71² ≈ 0.50: about half the variation in scores across the five models is accounted for by the straight-line relationship with coverage, and about half is not. That “not” half is Model E and everything else that differs between models — size, training, prompting, and luck.

The benchmark’s own figures, on all nineteen models, are sharper:

  • Variable coverage against score: r = 0.73, so r² = 0.73² ≈ 0.53. About half the variation in scores is accounted for by coverage.
  • Clarification rate against score: r = 0.40, so r² = 0.40² = 0.16. Only about a sixth of the variation in scores is accounted for by how often a model asks.

That contrast is the episode’s finding in one pair of numbers. Coverage accounts for roughly half the score variation; asking frequency accounts for roughly a sixth. A model that asks more often does not, on this evidence, score much better for asking more often. A model that asks about the right variables does.

How Sure Are We? The Fisher-z Interval for r

A correlation computed from nineteen models is itself an estimate, and it needs an interval, just as a pass rate does (Episode 6). The interval for r is not built directly on r, because r is squeezed into the −1 to +1 range and its sampling variation is lopsided near the ends. The standard route is the Fisher-z transformation: stretch r out with the inverse hyperbolic tangent, build an ordinary interval there, then squeeze back.

  • Step 1, transform: z = atanh(r) = ½ × ln((1 + r) ÷ (1 − r)). For r = 0.73: z = ½ × ln(1.73 ÷ 0.27) = ½ × ln(6.41) = ½ × 1.86 ≈ 0.93.
  • Step 2, standard error: SE(z) = 1 ÷ √(n − 3), where n is the number of models. For n = 19: SE = 1 ÷ √16 = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25.
  • Step 3, interval on z: 0.93 ± 1.96 × 0.25 = 0.93 ± 0.49, giving 0.44 to 1.42.
  • Step 4, transform back: r = tanh(z) = (e2z − 1) ÷ (e2z + 1). tanh(0.44) ≈ 0.41 and tanh(1.42) ≈ 0.89.

So the 95% interval for the coverage correlation is roughly 0.41 to 0.89. The whole interval sits well above zero: even at its low end, the relationship is substantial. Apply the same four steps to the clarification-rate correlation, r = 0.40 on the same nineteen models: z = atanh(0.40) ≈ 0.42, the interval on z is 0.42 ± 0.49, giving −0.07 to 0.91, and transforming back gives roughly −0.07 to 0.72. That interval includes zero and stretches from slightly negative to strongly positive. On nineteen models, the clarification-rate correlation is not pinned down at all: the data are consistent with no relationship, and also consistent with a strong one.

The five-model example is worse still. For r = 0.71 on n = 5: z ≈ 0.89, SE = 1 ÷ √2 ≈ 0.71, the interval on z is 0.89 ± 1.39, giving −0.50 to 2.28, and transforming back gives roughly −0.46 to 0.98. Five models cannot support a correlation claim. The five-model table teaches the arithmetic; the nineteen-model interval is the evidence.

Spearman’s ρ: When the Relationship Is Not a Straight Line

Pearson’s r asks whether two variables rise together in a straight line. Spearman’s ρ (the Greek letter rho) asks a looser question: whether they rise together at all, in any steady order. It does this by replacing each value with its rank — first, second, third — and then computing Pearson’s r on the ranks. When there are no tied values, there is a short formula:

ρ = 1 − (6 × Σd²) ÷ (n × (n² − 1))

Here d is the difference between the two ranks for one model, Σd² is the sum of the squared rank differences, and n is the number of models. Use Spearman’s ρ when the relationship looks steady but curved (a small gain in coverage buys a large gain in score at first, then levels off), when one outlying model would drag Pearson’s r around, or when the data are ranks or ordered ratings rather than measured numbers.

Σd² = 0 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 6. Then ρ = 1 − (6 × 6) ÷ (5 × (25 − 1)) = 1 − 36 ÷ 120 = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70. Spearman’s ρ of 0.70 is close to Pearson’s r of 0.71 here, which is what you expect when the relationship is roughly straight and no single model dominates. When the two disagree sharply, that disagreement is itself information: it says the relationship is ordered but not straight, or that an outlier is doing the work in Pearson’s r. Report both when they differ, and say which one you are using and why.

A Correlation Is Not a Cause

Variable coverage correlates with score at r = 0.73. Four explanations fit that number, and the correlation alone cannot choose between them:

  • Coverage improves the score. Asking about the right facts gives the model the information it needs, so its answers get better. This is the explanation the benchmark’s design points toward: the hidden facts are the ones the answer depends on.
  • A higher score produces coverage. A stronger model both answers better and asks better questions, so coverage is a symptom of capability rather than its source.
  • Something else drives both. Model size, training data, or prompting quality could raise both coverage and score together, with neither causing the other.
  • The pattern is a selection effect. Nineteen models from a small number of related model families are not nineteen independent draws. Models in one family share training, architecture, and prompting, so their results travel together (the same clustering problem as Episode 13, applied to models instead of documents). A correlation across a small number of related families is fragile: one family behaving differently can move it a long way.

The same reasoning applies to the familiar benchmark question, “does a bigger model mean a better score?” A correlation between model size and score across a benchmark’s model list shows that bigger models in that list scored higher. It does not show that making a model bigger raises its score, because size travels with training budget, data quality, and release date. To test the causal claim you need a comparison that holds those other things fixed — the same model family at different sizes, or an experiment — not a correlation across a mixed list.

None of this makes the correlation useless. A correlation is a lead: it says where to look next. Coverage is a better lead than clarification rate, because its correlation is stronger, its interval excludes zero, and it has a mechanism behind it — the questions target the facts the answer needs. The next step is a test of that mechanism, for example comparing the same model with its questions restricted to the variables that matter against the same model asking freely. The correlation tells you which test to run. It does not replace the test.

The Use Case

A vendor report or a benchmark paper claims a correlation: more context retrieved means better answers, longer reasoning means better drafting, bigger models mean better scores. Before accepting the claim, ask four questions:

  • What are the two variables, exactly, and what are their units? “Asking more” can mean clarification rate or variable coverage, and the two give different correlations.
  • How many points is the correlation computed from, and are they independent? Nineteen models from a handful of families carry less information than nineteen independent models.
  • Where is the interval? A correlation of 0.40 on nineteen models has an interval from roughly −0.07 to 0.72: it does not establish a relationship at all.
  • What mechanism is proposed, and what would test it? A correlation with no mechanism and no follow-up test is a lead, not a finding.

Apply the same four questions to your own evals. If you are comparing five builds on one test set, a correlation across the five builds is a description of those five builds, not evidence about builds in general. Say so in the report, and put the scatter plot beside the number so the reader can see Model E for themselves.

Conclusion

What the model asks matters more than how often it asks. And a correlation is not a cause. Coverage correlates with score at r = 0.73, with an interval of roughly 0.41 to 0.89 on nineteen models; clarification rate correlates at r = 0.40, with an interval that includes zero. The numbers point at coverage. The proof would be a test, not a bigger correlation.
