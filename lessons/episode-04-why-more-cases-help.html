Episode 4 — Why More Cases Help

Episode 4 · Module 1 — Foundations: What a Number Means · Audio 6:30 · Statistics for Legal Evals

Audio

The legal department has run the contract platform on ten contracts and has a mean score. The vendor offers to run forty more. Is that worth the time? The department is deciding whether a ten-case pilot tells it anything at all about the platform’s performance — and, if not, how many cases would.

The question this episode answers: why is 50 questions steadier than 5? The answer is the sampling distribution, the standard error, and the Central Limit Theorem — the whole reason test sets work.

One Question Wobbles; a Mean Steadies

Take one contract, scored by the platform. From Episode 1, the ten pilot scores had a population standard deviation of σ = 2.45. A single score can sit a long way from the mean: the observed scores ran from 4 to 10 around a mean of 7. One document tells you about that document. It tells you very little about the platform.

Now take the mean of 50 contracts. Each individual score still varies as much as before, but the high scores and the low scores partly cancel each other out in the average. A mean of 50 is steadier than a single score, because the wobble in one direction is offset by wobble in the other. The more items in the mean, the more cancelling happens, and the steadier the mean becomes. That is the whole reason test sets work: averages of many things are steadier than single things.

The Sampling Distribution

Imagine running the pilot many times: draw 50 contracts, compute the mean, write it down; draw another 50, compute the mean, write it down; and so on, hundreds of times. The list of means you collect has its own distribution. That distribution is called the sampling distribution of the mean. It is a distribution of averages, not a distribution of individual scores.

The sampling distribution has a centre and a spread, like any distribution. Its centre is the true mean: the average the platform would score if it were run on every contract in the population. Its spread is narrower than the spread of individual scores, because averages vary less than single items do. The spread of the sampling distribution is what the standard error measures.

Figure 1. The sampling distribution of the mean, for samples of 5, 20, and 50 items. All three are centred on the same true mean. As n grows, the distribution narrows: the mean of a larger sample varies less from sample to sample.

The Standard Error: σ ÷ √n

The standard error, written SE, is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. It measures how far a sample mean typically sits from the true mean. Its formula is:

SE = σ ÷ √n

Here σ is the standard deviation of individual scores — how much single items vary — and n is the number of items in the sample. The square root is the key part. The standard error shrinks as one over the square root of n. Double the sample and the standard error shrinks by a factor of √2, about 1.41, not by half. To halve the standard error, the sample must grow fourfold: four times the cases, half the wobble.

The worked numbers below use σ = 2.45, the population standard deviation of the ten pilot scores from Episode 1. That is a teaching simplification, stated explicitly: the ten pilot scores are being used as if their spread were the spread of individual scores in the wider population. The arithmetic is what matters here, and it is shown in full.

Items in the mean (n) Standard error Working Approximate 95% range for the mean
1 2.45 2.45 ÷ √1 = 2.45 mean ± 4.80
5 1.10 2.45 ÷ √5 = 2.45 ÷ 2.24 = 1.10 mean ± 2.15
10 0.77 2.45 ÷ √10 = 2.45 ÷ 3.16 = 0.77 mean ± 1.52
20 0.55 2.45 ÷ √20 = 2.45 ÷ 4.47 = 0.55 mean ± 1.07
50 0.35 2.45 ÷ √50 = 2.45 ÷ 7.07 = 0.35 mean ± 0.68
200 0.17 2.45 ÷ √200 = 2.45 ÷ 14.14 = 0.17 mean ± 0.34

Read the table from the top. With one item, the standard error is 2.45 — the mean of one item is just that item, so it wobbles as much as a single score. With five items, the standard error is 1.10. With 50 items, it is 0.35. With 200 items, it is 0.17. The fourfold rule is visible in the table: from n = 5 to n = 20, the standard error halves, from 1.10 to 0.55. From n = 50 to n = 200, it halves again, from 0.35 to 0.17.

The last column is the approximate 95% range: the sample mean, plus or minus 1.96 times the standard error. The multiplier 1.96 comes from the normal distribution, and is explained in the next section. With 50 items, the mean sits within about 0.68 points of the true mean in about 95% of samples. With five items, that range is 2.15 points — more than three times as wide. A ten-case pilot with a mean of 7.0 and a standard error of 0.77 has an approximate 95% range of 7.0 ± 1.52, or about 5.5 to 8.5. That range is the answer to whether a ten-case pilot tells the department anything: it tells the department the mean is somewhere in a three-point range, which is a start, and not a precise estimate.

The standard error is not the standard deviation. The standard deviation, σ, describes how much individual scores vary. The standard error, σ ÷ √n, describes how much sample means vary. Confusing the two is a common error in pilot reports: a report that quotes the standard deviation of the scores as if it were the uncertainty in the mean overstates the uncertainty by a factor of √n.

The Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem says that the sampling distribution of the mean becomes approximately normal — bell-shaped, symmetric around the true mean — as the sample size grows, whatever the distribution of the individual scores looks like. The ten pilot scores from Episode 1 were bimodal, with two clusters and a gap in the middle. The mean of 50 such scores is still approximately normal. That is the theorem’s force: the average smooths out the irregularities of the individual items.

The theorem needs three conditions. First, the items must be independent: each item’s score must not depend on the others. Second, the sample must be large enough for the approximation to be good. How large is large enough depends on the distribution of the individual scores: for roughly symmetric scores, a few dozen items are enough; for heavily skewed scores, more are needed. Third, the individual scores must have a finite variance — a condition that holds for scores on a bounded ten-point scale.

The approximation is weak at small n. The 95% range for n = 5 in the table above uses the normal multiplier 1.96, but with five items drawn from a bimodal distribution the sampling distribution is not yet close to normal, so that range is rough. By n = 50 it is a good approximation. The lesson is not that small samples are useless, but that the normal approximation — and the intervals built on it — should not be trusted at small n without checking. Episode 6 builds proper intervals for small samples.

The multiplier 1.96 is the point on a standard normal distribution that leaves 2.5% in each tail, so that 95% of the distribution lies within 1.96 standard errors of the centre. It is a property of the normal distribution, not of the data. The Central Limit Theorem is what licenses using it for a sample mean.

What the Standard Error Does Not Do

The standard error shrinks random error only. It says nothing about bias. A convenience sample of easy contracts stays biased at any n: the mean of 500 template NDAs is a precise estimate of performance on template NDAs, and it still says nothing about bespoke vendor contracts. That is Episode 3’s lesson, and it is the reason sample quality comes before sample size. More cases make a biased estimate more precise, not less biased.

The standard error also says nothing about clustering. Every n in this episode assumes independent items: each contract’s score independent of the others. If ten questions come from one contract, or twenty clauses from one MSA, the items travel together, and the effective sample size is smaller than the headline n. Episode 13 works through the design effect that corrects for clustering. Until then, treat every n in this episode as a count of independent items.

Finally, the standard error describes the uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of the scores. A small standard error does not mean the platform’s scores are consistent with each other. It means the average is precisely estimated. The platform can score 4 on some documents and 10 on others — a wide spread, σ = 2.45 — while the mean of 50 documents is estimated to within 0.35 points. Both statements are true at once, and they answer different questions.

The Use Case: Sizing a Pilot

The department is deciding whether a ten-case pilot tells it anything at all. With σ = 2.45, a ten-case pilot gives a standard error of 0.77 and an approximate 95% range of about 5.5 to 8.5 around a mean of 7.0. That range is wide enough to matter: a platform whose true mean is 5.5 and a platform whose true mean is 8.5 are different platforms, and the pilot cannot tell them apart. The pilot is not useless — it can reveal a split between document types, as Episode 1 showed, and it can reveal catastrophic failures — but it cannot pin down the mean.

To halve the range, the department needs four times the cases: 40 contracts instead of 10, giving a standard error of about 0.39 and a range of about 7.0 ± 0.76. To halve it again, 160 contracts. Each halving costs four times as much testing. The decision is therefore a trade-off between precision and cost, and the standard error is what prices it. Episode 9 takes up the full sizing question: how many cases are needed to estimate a score, to detect a difference, and to compare two builds.

Checklist: Reading a Mean and Its Uncertainty

  • Ask for n: how many independent items went into the mean?
  • Ask for the standard error, not just the standard deviation. The standard error is σ ÷ √n.
  • Check the fourfold rule: halving the wobble needs four times the cases.
  • Ask whether the items are independent. Items from the same document travel together.
  • Ask whether the sample is representative. A precise estimate of the wrong population is still the wrong population.
  • Ask for the interval around the mean, not just the mean. The width is the news.

Conclusion

Averages of many things are steadier than single things. That is the whole reason test sets work. The standard error, σ ÷ √n, says how much steadier: four times the cases, half the wobble. It shrinks random error only. Bias and clustering are separate problems, and they need separate fixes.
