19 episodes in 5 modules. One technique per episode.

Module 1 — Foundations: What a Number Means

Episode 1 — What a Number Means

The question
"We scored 70%. What does that even mean?"

Technique
Mean, median, mode, spread (range, standard deviation), histograms, bimodal distributions, comparing two builds with the same mean, skew (mean vs median), stratification and post-stratification.

Legal evals use case
Summarising a pilot eval for a partner; spotting that a build fails one document type entirely while looking fine on average.

Remember
One average can hide two different stories.

Episode 2 — Base Rates and Bayes

The question
"The model says 90% likely. Likely compared to what?"

Technique
Probability, conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, base rates.

Legal evals use case
Reading a vendor's accuracy claim; judging whether a triage check is doing anything.

Remember
Every probability needs a base rate. Without one, the number is a slogan.

Episode 3 — How Do You Build a Test Set?

The question
"How do I know my test set is fair?"

Technique
Sampling frames, random vs convenience vs stratified sampling, oversampling rare categories and weighting back, development/evaluation separation, leakage and contamination, label noise and the human noise floor, the unit of evaluation, model/product/workflow evaluation levels.

Legal evals use case
Building a test set before running a pilot; judging a vendor's test set.

Remember
More examples cannot repair an unrepresentative test set. Teach sample quality alongside sample size.

Episode 4 — Why More Cases Help

The question
"Why is 50 questions steadier than 5?"

Technique
Sampling distribution, standard error, the Central Limit Theorem.

Legal evals use case
Deciding whether a ten-case pilot tells you anything at all.

Remember
Averages of many things are steadier than single things. That is the whole reason test sets work.

Episode 5 — Pass or Fail Is a Weighted Coin

The question
"We got 7 out of 10. Is that good?"

Technique
The binomial distribution, expected counts, how much counts vary by chance.

Legal evals use case
Reading a pass rate from a small test set.

Remember
Counts wobble. 7 out of 10 is evidence, not a verdict.

Module 2 — The Inference Toolkit

Episode 6 — How Sure Are You?

The question
"7 out of 10 — what range is the truth actually in?"

Technique
Confidence intervals; the Wilson interval; why the Wald interval is wrong at small n; the 4x rule; the rule of three and Clopper–Pearson intervals for rare events and zero failures.

Legal evals use case
Reporting a pilot score to a partner without overstating it; reading a vendor's "zero failures" claim.

Remember
Report the interval, never the bare percentage. The width is the news.

Episode 7 — What Does p Mean?

The question
"The vendor says p = 0.03. What are they actually claiming?"

Technique
The p-value — the probability of seeing data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. The common misreadings: it is not the probability the null is true, and not the probability your fix worked.

Legal evals use case
Judging whether a prompt fix actually helped, or whether the difference is consistent with chance.

Remember
p is a statement about the data under an assumption, not about the truth of your claim.

Episode 8 — What Did the Tool Miss?

The question
"70% accurate — but what did it miss, and what did it flag for nothing?"

Technique
The confusion matrix; precision, recall, F1; specificity; positive and negative predictive values; prevalence; why accuracy misleads when the thing that matters is rare. Retrieval metrics: precision@k, recall@k (mean reciprocal rank and nDCG on the lesson page as a sidebar).

Legal evals use case
Reading any vendor scorecard; judging an extraction or review tool where the misses matter more than the hits.

Remember
Accuracy hides the asymmetry. Precision and recall show it. Name the denominator.

Episode 9 — How Many Cases Do You Need?

The question
"How many test cases before I can believe the result?"

Technique
Power; the three questions (estimate, detect, compare); sample-size tables; effect size beats sample size.

Legal evals use case
Sizing a test set before running it — and the correction that significance is the wrong goal for a ten-question teaching eval.

Remember
"How many cases?" is three questions, not one. Answer the wrong one and the number is useless.

Episode 10 — How Big Is the Difference?

The question
"It's significant. Is it worth anything?"

Technique
Effect size; the minimum important difference set by the department; Cohen's d for means (short aside only); the raw point difference for proportions; significance is not importance; non-inferiority testing (equivalence / TOST as a separate written aside).

Legal evals use case
Deciding whether to ship a fix, or to keep tuning; deciding whether a cheaper model is acceptably worse.

Remember
A p-value says how surprising the data are under an assumption. Effect size says "big enough to care about." You need both — and the margin is yours to set, not the vendor's.

Module 3 — Comparing Builds

Episode 11 — The Paired t-Test

The question
"Build A averages 0.82, Build B 0.76. Is the gap real?"

Technique
The paired t-test; the t-distribution and why small samples need it; when to use it instead of McNemar; the Wilcoxon signed-rank fallback when differences are not roughly normal; the bootstrap / permutation interval alongside.

Legal evals use case
Mean rubric scores, mean Brier scores, mean cost or latency per task — any metric that is a mean over items.

Remember
Pass/fail is McNemar's territory. Means are the t-test's. Use the test that matches the metric.

Episode 12 — Did the Fix Help?

The question
"Same questions, new prompt. Better?"

Technique
McNemar's exact test; the paired bootstrap; why pairing buys power over unpaired comparison (the gain depends on the correlation between the paired measurements — there is no universal multiplier).

Legal evals use case
Before/after on the same test set — the most common comparison in legal evals.

Remember
Same questions, paired test. Different questions, you have thrown away your best evidence.

Episode 13 — Your Items Are Not Independent

The question
"We have 100 questions. Do we really?"

Technique
Clustering; the design effect, 1 + (m − 1)ρ; effective sample size; the cluster bootstrap; stratified resampling; run-to-run variance as a second cluster.

Legal evals use case
Test sets built from a few documents — ten questions per judgment, twenty clauses per contract — where the headline n overstates the confidence; repeated model runs on the same items.

Remember
Questions from the same document travel together. Count documents, not just questions — and count runs, not just items.

Module 4 — Scoring and Agreement

Episode 14 — Scoring Probabilities

The question
"The model says 90% confident. Is it right 90% of the time?"

Technique
The Brier score; the Murphy decomposition (reliability, resolution, uncertainty); calibration; expected calibration error (ECE); the 90%-confidence bucket check; log loss; reliability diagrams; ROC-AUC and PR-AUC — discrimination vs calibration.

Legal evals use case
Any model that gives probabilities — dismissal odds, risk scores, settlement ranges.

Remember
Accuracy says how often it is right. Calibration says whether its confidence means anything. Check both — and check whether it separates, too.

Episode 15 — Do the Graders Agree?

The question
"Two partners graded the same outputs. Did they agree?"

Technique
Cohen's κ (two raters); Fleiss κ (three or more); weighted κ for ordinal ratings; intraclass correlation (ICC) for continuous scores; Krippendorff's α — with a guide to when each fits; choosing a judge panel by κ with held-out validation; agreement is not correctness; LLM-judge bias (position, length, self-preference).

Legal evals use case
Human grading of eval outputs; picking an AI judge panel.

Remember
Raw agreement flatters. Kappa asks how much agreement survives chance — and agreement is not correctness.

Episode 16 — Did the Triage Check Work?

The question
"The model asks for more facts when it should — and when it shouldn't?"

Technique
True positive rate, false positive rate, balanced accuracy; the threshold trade-off; ROC and precision–recall curves; cost-weighted decisions and expected cost; abstention / non-conclusions as a third outcome.

Legal evals use case
Any triage step that routes work — answer vs ask, review vs escalate.

Remember
A check has two error rates, not one. Report both, or the number is a lie by omission — and price the errors, or the threshold is a guess.

Episode 17 — What the Model Asks Matters More

The question
"Models that ask more do better. Or do they?"

Technique
Pearson's r, r-squared, the Fisher-z confidence interval for r, correlation versus causation; Spearman's ρ as the alternative when relationships are not linear.

Legal evals use case
Reading a benchmark's correlation claims; "does a bigger model mean a better score?"

Remember
What the model asks matters more than how often it asks. And a correlation is not a cause.

Module 5 — Reporting Your Results

Episode 18 — How Not to Fool Yourself

The question
"We ran five tests and one came out significant. Did we find something?"

Technique
P-hacking; multiple comparisons; the Bonferroni correction; the Holm correction and Benjamini–Hochberg; pre-specification; overlapping confidence intervals; subgroup analysis and multiplicity.

Legal evals use case
Reading a vendor report that tested everything and reported the one hit; running your own pilot without fooling yourself.

Remember
If you test enough things, something will look real. Decide the tests before you look.

Episode 19 — Reporting Your Results (Series Finale)

The question
"How do I write this up so a reader can see exactly what it proves — and what it doesn't?"

Technique
The one-page eval report; cost and the Pareto frontier; post-deployment monitoring (weekly spot-checks, control charts, drift); workflow evaluation (lawyer-only vs lawyer-plus-AI); measurement validity; versioning; the cautionary tales.

Legal evals use case
Writing up an eval for a partner, a vendor, or a regulator.

Remember
A report that hides its limits is a demo with better stationery.
