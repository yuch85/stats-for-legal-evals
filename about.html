About

Statistics for Legal Evals is a podcast series that teaches lawyers statistical techniques for legal evals. It is lawyer-curated for educational purposes only, does not represent the views of any organisation, and does not constitute legal advice.

The audience is practising lawyers who know nothing about statistics. No maths background and no coding are assumed. Every technique is taught through a specific legal evals use case, with the worked numbers shown.

How the series works

One fictional product runs through all 19 episodes: a legal AI contract intelligence platform used by a legal innovation team to review commercial agreements against company playbooks. Each episode introduces a new technique because the team meets a new question. The scores and cases in the worked examples are the series’ own illustrative values unless an episode names a published source.

Each episode has an audio version of about seven minutes, a video with slides (videos are being added), and a written lesson page that goes deeper than the audio: formulas, worked numbers, edge cases and sources.

The hosts

Sera and Ben are the two hosts. Their voices are AI-generated, and the audio files are tagged as such.

Episode 1: What a Number Means

  • The ten-score worked example in this episode is the series’ own: five NDAs (9, 9, 10, 9, 10) and five MSAs (5, 4, 5, 4, 5); mean 7.0, median 7, modes 5 and 9, range 4–10, population standard deviation 2.45 (sample standard deviation 2.58). The second build’s ten scores (6, 7, 7, 8, 7, 6, 8, 7, 7, 6) are also the series’ own: mean 6.9, median 7, mode 7, range 6–8, population standard deviation 0.70 (sample standard deviation 0.74). Build B by type: NDAs 8, 8, 7, 7, 7 (mean 7.4), MSAs 7, 7, 6, 6, 6 (mean 6.4). Portfolio-weighted (20% NDA, 80% MSA): Build A 5.56, Build B 6.60. Illustrative evaluation dataset, not actual vendor performance data.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 2–3 — mean, median, mode, standard deviation, and histograms, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 2: Base Rates and Bayes

  • LegalForecastBench — repository: github.com/johnhughes3/LegalForecastBench. Code Apache 2.0, data CC BY 4.0. The repository is the source for the benchmark’s design, the claim–defendant unit definition, the 91 cases and 387 units, and the micro-Brier metric; it links to the working paper and results site for the figures (73.6% dismissal base rate, 0.118 best-model Brier). Discussed here with attribution; no test data reproduced.
  • SG-LegalClarify — Gu et al., arXiv:2611.19643. 1,100 lawyer-authored Singapore-law questions (340 fully specified, 760 underspecified, 1,860 instances); best balanced check accuracy 69.4%. Discussed here with attribution; no questions reproduced. The sufficiency check figures used here (Luna TPR 89.3%, FPR 64.1%; Mistral TPR 16.2%, FPR 3.5%) are as reported for the benchmark; the 200-question worked set (100 needing facts, 100 not) is the series’ own teaching construction built from those rates.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 3 and ch. 4 — conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 3: How Do You Build a Test Set?

  • The pilot test-set design in this episode is the series’ own worked example: a sampling frame of 50 contracts, stratified by type, with indemnity-heavy MSAs oversampled and results weighted back to the portfolio mix; the prompt tuned on 30 contracts, tested on the untouched 20; two lawyers disagreeing on 8 of the 50 scores (16%). Illustrative evaluation design, not actual vendor performance data.
  • NIST AI 800-2, Practices for Automated Benchmark Evaluations, initial public draft, January 2026 — representative test sets, item sampling, and nondeterministic completions as uncertainty sources. Initial public draft, not a final standard.
  • NIST AI 200-2, TEVV-Athlon, initial public draft, August 2026 — evaluation practice for AI systems. Initial public draft, not a final standard.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 1 — sampling frames, random sampling, and stratified sampling, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 4: Why More Cases Help

  • The worked numbers in this episode are the series’ own, computed from the Episode 1 pilot scores (population standard deviation σ = 2.45). The use of σ = 2.45 as the spread of individual scores in the wider population is a teaching simplification, stated in the text. Standard errors: n = 1, 2.45; n = 5, 1.10; n = 10, 0.77; n = 20, 0.55; n = 50, 0.35; n = 200, 0.17. Approximate 95% ranges use the normal multiplier 1.96.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 7 — the Central Limit Theorem and the sampling distribution of the mean, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 5: Pass or Fail Is a Weighted Coin

  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 4 — the binomial distribution, expected value and standard deviation of a count, the standard undergraduate treatment.
  • The worked example in this episode is the series’ own: 7 passes out of 10 clauses under true pass rates of 50% and 80%. P(X = 7) = 120 × 0.510 ≈ 0.117 under the 50% build; 120 × 0.87 × 0.23 ≈ 0.201 under the 80% build. P(X ≥ 7) ≈ 0.172 and 0.879 respectively; P(X = 7) under a 30% build ≈ 0.009.
  • Feller, W., An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications, vol. 1, 3rd ed., ch. 6 — the binomial distribution and its approximations.

Episode 6: How Sure Are You?

  • Wilson, E. B. (1927). “Probable inference, the law of succession, and statistical inference.” Journal of the American Statistical Association 22(158), 209–212 — the Wilson interval.
  • Brown, L. D., Cai, T. T., & DasGupta, A. (2001). “Interval estimation for a binomial proportion.” Statistical Science 16(2), 101–133 — the Wald interval’s poor coverage at small n, and the recommendation to use the Wilson interval instead.
  • Clopper, C. J., & Pearson, E. S. (1934). “The use of confidence or fiducial limits illustrated in the case of the binomial.” Biometrika 26(4), 404–413 — the exact (Clopper–Pearson) interval.
  • Agresti, A., & Coull, B. A. (1998). “Approximate is better than ‘exact’ for interval estimation of binomial proportions.” The American Statistician 52(2), 119–126.
  • The worked examples in this episode are the series’ own: 7/10 gives a 95% Wilson interval of 39.7–89.2% and a Wald interval of 41.6–98.4%; 0/20 gives a rule-of-three upper bound of 15%, a Wilson upper bound of 16.1% and a Clopper–Pearson upper bound of 13.9%.
  • Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (S.D.N.Y. 2023) — fabricated citations in a filing. Dahl, M., et al. (2024). “Large legal fictions: Profiling legal hallucinations in large language models.” Stanford RegLab / Human-Centered AI — measured hallucination rates in commercial legal research tools.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 8 — confidence intervals for a proportion, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 7: What Does p Mean?

  • Wasserstein, R. L., & Lazar, N. A. (2016). “The ASA statement on statistical significance and p-values.” The American Statistician 70(2), 129–133 — the p-value’s definition and its common misreadings.
  • Fisher, R. A. (1925). Statistical Methods for Research Workers. Oliver & Boyd — the origin of the 5% threshold as a convention.
  • The worked example in this episode is the series’ own: 68 passes out of 100 against a claimed true rate of 75%. The one-sided exact binomial p-value is P(X ≤ 68) ≈ 0.069; the Wilson 95% interval for 68/100 is 58.3–76.3%. P(X ≤ 68) is about 0.367 if the true rate were 70%, and about 0.003 if it were 80%.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 9 — hypothesis testing for a single proportion, the standard undergraduate treatment.

Episode 8: What Did the Tool Miss?

  • Grossman, M. R., & Cormack, G. V. (2011). “Technology-assisted review in e-discovery can be more effective and more efficient than exhaustive manual review.” Richmond Journal of Law & Technology 17(3) — the elusion and recall estimation method for TAR validation.
  • Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe, 287 F.R.D. 182 (S.D.N.Y. 2012) — court approval of a technology-assisted review protocol. Deutsche Bank AG v Chang Tse Wen [2010] SGHC 125 — the Singapore court ordering a party to follow the e-discovery protocol.
  • Dahl, M., et al. (2024). “Large legal fictions: Profiling legal hallucinations in large language models.” Stanford RegLab / Human-Centered AI — measured hallucination rates in commercial legal research tools, by task and by denominator.
  • Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (S.D.N.Y. 2023) — fabricated citations in a filing.
  • The worked examples in this episode are the series’ own: the 200-clause confusion matrix (TP 60, FP 40, FN 20, TN 80; precision 60%, recall 75%, F1 67%, accuracy 70%, specificity 67%); the privilege review at 95% sensitivity, 90% specificity and 3% prevalence (PPV about 23%, NPV about 99.8%); the TAR elusion sample (12 of 400, Wilson interval about 1.7–5.2%, estimated 270 missed documents, estimated recall about 79% with an interval of roughly 68–87%); the retrieval example (precision@10 60%, recall@10 40%) and the nDCG example (DCG 4.49, IDCG 4.76, nDCG about 0.94).
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 8 — the Wilson interval used for the elusion interval.

Episode 9: How Many Cases Do You Need?

Biometrics, 43(1), 207–211 — the normal approximation for the paired comparison used in this episode; the source of the about-625 total paired cases figure at 80% power, two-sided α = 0.05, 20% discordant rate, 5-point difference.
  • Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences, 2nd ed. — power, effect size, and the conventions (α = 0.05, power 0.80) used throughout this episode.
  • Chow, S.-C., Shao, J., Wang, H., & Lokhnygina, Y. (2017). Sample Size Calculations in Clinical Research, 3rd ed. — sample-size formulas for estimation and for paired proportions, including the exact conditional calculation.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 8 — the estimation formula n = z² p(1 − p) ÷ E² and the ±10-point / ±5-point worked values.
  • The estimation ranges (62–97 cases for ±10 points; 246–385 cases for ±5 points) and the effect-size table are the series’ own worked calculations from the formulas above, at 95% confidence.

Episode 12: Did the Fix Help?

Episode 17: What the Model Asks Matters More

  • Gu et al., SG-LegalClarify, arXiv:2611.19643 — 1,100 lawyer-authored Singapore-law questions (340 fully specified, 760 underspecified, 1,860 instances), 19 large language models tested closed-book and with web search; variable coverage correlates with score at r = 0.73, clarification rate at r = 0.40. Numbers cited and discussed; the benchmark’s questions are not reproduced.
  • The five-model worked example in this episode is the series’ own illustrative teaching set: coverage 40, 55, 65, 75, 90 against scores 55, 60, 79, 84, 72; r ≈ 0.71, Spearman’s ρ = 0.70. The Fisher-z intervals (r = 0.73 on 19 models, roughly 0.41–0.89; r = 0.40 on 19 models, roughly −0.07 to 0.72; r = 0.71 on 5 models, roughly −0.46 to 0.98) are computed from those figures by the method shown.
  • OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2nd ed., ch. 12 — Pearson’s r, r-squared, and the Fisher-z interval; Spearman’s rank correlation as the ordinal alternative.
  • Fisher, R. A. (1921), “On the probable error of a coefficient of correlation deduced from a small sample”, Metron 1 — the Fisher-z transformation for the interval on a correlation.
